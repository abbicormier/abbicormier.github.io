Hats, Greek letters, and where the variables go

You asked

"How do I keep things separate and remember the letters, order, etc.?"

The cast of characters

You seeRead it asExample
ythe outcome: what we're explaininglog wage
x (or x1, when we have a multivariate regression)the variable we care aboutyears of school
zsomething left out of the regressionability
β0, β1 (no hat)the true intercept and slope. We never see them.the estimand
β1 (hat)our estimate of β1, computed from the sample0.052
uthe leftover: everything else that affects yluck in the job search, a generous boss, …
ŷthe line's prediction$17 for 12 years
y − ŷ (or û)the residual: actual outcome minus predicted outcome value20 − 17 = 3
SEhow much β would bounce from sample to sample0.0029
t(estimate − hypothesized value of the estimand) / SE0.052 / 0.0029 ≈ 18

Four rules

  1. A hat means "computed from our sample." No hat means the true number we're after.
  2. Writing the model, before any data: no hats, and include u.
    y = β0 + β1x + u
  3. Plugging in numbers from output: put on hats, and drop u, because the fitted line gives predictions.
    ŷ = 5.807 + 0.044 exper + 0.321 college
  4. Greek letters are name tags. β, δ, α and γ each mean "some unknown number (coefficient) that multiplies this variable." Day 5 used δ for the dummies only to tell them apart. What matters is which variable a letter multiplies. The order of the terms doesn't matter either, though in different cases there will be a normal way to do it.

From a word question to an equation

  1. What are we explaining? That's y, on the left.
  2. "The effect of…", "the return to…", "how much does ___ go with…" names the main x. Its coefficient is the answer.
  3. "Holding ___ constant", "controlling for ___", "among people with the same ___" names more x's, typically potential confounders we want to be sure to include.
  4. "Differs between groups" means a dummy. "Does the effect of x differ between groups?" means an interaction (x × dummy).
  5. Asked in percent? Use log y as the outcome, and read 100 × coefficient as a percent.

What a residual means

You asked

"Going back over residuals. Less the actual calculation, more what it means / how it translates to an equation."

A residual answers one question about one person: how far is this person above or below what the line predicts for someone like them? Click a worker from Day 1 to read their residual as a sentence.

In an equation, every data point splits into the line's guess plus the miss: y = ŷ + residual. For worker B, 20 = 17 + 3.

The model has the same split: y = β0 + β1x + u. There, u is the true leftover: everything besides school that sets this man's wage. We never see u, so the residual is our sample's stand-in for it (that's why it's sometimes written û).

Residuals show up all through the unit. OLS picks the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals (Day 1). FWL works with "residualized" schooling (Day 2). Robust standard errors use the residuals' spread (Day 4).

Check yourself. A worker with 14 years of school earns $26 an hour. What is the residual, and what does it say?

Show answer

The line predicts −19 + 3 × 14 = $23. The residual is 26 − 23 = +3: this worker earns $3 an hour more than the line expects for 14 years of school.

MSE: what the line is trying to do

You asked

"LMSE? On my notes, don't know what it means :/"

The handout's term is MSE, mean squared error. (The L is probably from "least," as in least squares.) It's the score from Day 1's first game: you guess, you lose the square of your miss, and the MSE is your average loss.

New example

Three men earn $10, $12 and $20 an hour. Pick one guess of their hourly wage, the same number for all three.

$8$17$22

Squaring makes every miss count and big misses count more, and the guess that keeps the MSE smallest is the average. OLS does the same thing with a line instead of one number: it picks the line with the least squared error. That's all "least squares" means.

Check yourself. If the three wages were $10, $12 and $50, what guess gives the smallest MSE?

Show answer

The average, $24. One big wage pulls the best guess a long way, because its miss gets squared.

Holding things constant, and the empty groups

You asked

"Day 1, Question 5: What does it mean to hold things constant in regression, and how does that solve the empty groups problem? What is FWL?"

Holding experience constant means comparing men who have the same experience, so a schooling difference isn't mixed up with an experience difference.

The direct way is matching: sort men into groups that match on everything else, then compare schooling within each group. With Card's controls that takes 3,456 groups for 3,010 men, and only 686 of the groups have anyone in them. There's nobody to compare with.

Regression doesn't need exact matches. Instead it assumes a shape. In our case, we often assume each control shifts wages by a set amount (for example, living in the South shifts log wages by the same amount at every level of schooling). Then it estimates each shift from all the men at once, so every man helps estimate every coefficient.

That eases the empty-groups problem; it doesn't make it go away. Two separate problems are left:

  1. The shape can be wrong. If wages don't really shift by a set amount for each control, the adjustment is off.
  2. The data still get spread thinner. Each extra control that moves with schooling takes away some of the variation in schooling we learn from, so the estimate can get less precise (a bigger SE).
New example

To see what an extra bedroom adds to a home's price, you'd want houses that match on size, neighborhood and age. Most of those combinations have zero or one house. A regression instead assumes each extra 100 square feet adds the same amount everywhere, each extra year of age changes the price by the same amount everywhere, and each neighborhood has its own fixed shift. Then it estimates those from every house:

price = β0 + β1 bedrooms + β2 size + β3 age
   + δ1 nbhd1 + δ2 nbhd2 + … + u

size is in 100s of square feet, and each nbhd is a dummy for one neighborhood. β1 compares homes with the same size, age and neighborhood.

FWL is how regression does the holding constant. It's the first answer under Day 2.

Check yourself. Why can't we just compare men with exactly the same experience, region, race and city?

Show answer

Most of those groups are empty or hold one man, so for most men there's nobody with a different amount of school to compare with.

FWL: what does it even look like?

You asked

"Frisch–Waugh–Lovell Theorem explanation / question. What does that even look like? IDK! Help me please :("

"What is FWL?"

"Can you explain heteroskedasticity and FWL again and how they relate to regressions / why are they important?"

FWL says the coefficient on x1 in a regression with controls is the slope you get in two steps:

  1. Regress x1 on the controls, and keep the residuals. That line's prediction is the x1 that's typical for people with the same controls. The residual, r1 = x1 − (that prediction), is what's left: how much more or less x1 this person has than is typical for people like them. Whatever the controls can predict has been taken out of r1.
  2. Regress y on r1. That's a simple regression with one x, like Day 1. Its slope is exactly β1 from the regression with controls.

So FWL means we can write β1 from a multivariate regression with the Day 1 slope formula, using r1 in place of x:

β1 = cov(r1, y) / var(r1)

In one sentence: controlling for z means comparing x1 to its typical value for people with the same z.

Two thumbs (omitted variable bias): where the + and − come from

You asked

"Day 3, Problem 2: Two thumbs. How do you know what's negative and positive, like x & y & z? How do you get +, − from them?"

"What is the way to find correlation with direction for OVB? Another example would be helpful."

When a variable z is left out and has a relationship to y and to x, the slope on x picks up part of z's effect. The two thumbs, or thinking about the direction of those two relationships, tell you which way that pushes the slope. Each thumb answers one question with a sign:

  • Thumb 1, z → y. Picture two people with the same x. If one has more z, is their y higher (+) or lower (−)?
  • Thumb 2, z with x (the "correlation with direction"). In the data, do people with more x tend to have more z (+) or less z (−)? It's a tendency, not a cause.
  • Multiply. Same signs (+ +, or − −): the bias is up, so the slope without z is too big. Different signs: the bias is down, so it's too small.

That's Day 3's formula written as signs: slope without z = slope with z + (thumb 1) × (thumb 2). In the studying example, −1 = 2 + (−30) × (0.1). The −30 is thumb 1 (the hard class lowers scores at the same hours) and the 0.1 is thumb 2 (hard-class students study more). If you have the data, thumb 2's sign is the slope from regressing z on x.

New example

Does tutoring raise grades? Left out: being behind already. Thumb 1: of two students with the same tutoring hours, the one who was behind gets a lower grade (−). Thumb 2: students who are behind sign up for more tutoring (+). Different signs, so the bias is down: the plain slope understates tutoring and can even make it look harmful.

Sampling error: draw another sample

You asked

"Day 3: Can you go over sampling error: draw another sample. 5, 5a, 5b, 5c, 5d."

Even with no bias at all, the estimate isn't exactly the estimand, because we only have one sample. Draw a different 100 men and you get a different slope. The gap between a sample's estimate and the estimand is that sample's sampling error, and it changes from sample to sample.

  • 5a. Draw 1,000 samples of 100 men from Card's data. The slopes average 0.052 and spread out with an SD of 0.016.
  • 5b. That SD, the spread of the slope across samples, is the standard error: how much the estimate bounces from sample to sample. With 400 men per sample it's about 0.008. Four times the men, half the SE.
  • 5c. With all 3,010 men the SE is 0.0029, so the 95% confidence interval is 0.052 ± 1.96 × 0.0029 = 0.046 to 0.058.
  • 5d. "95%" describes the recipe, not this one interval: if we drew many samples and built an interval from each, about 95% of them would contain the true slope.
New example

Ask 100 random students how much they spend on coffee per week and you might get an average of $14. Ask a different 100 and you might get $12.50. Neither poll is biased; they differ by "luck," or sampling error. Ask 400 at a time and the averages land closer together.

Three terms that sound alike
  • Sampling error is one number for one sample: how far that sample's estimate lands from the estimand, just from the luck of who got drawn. It's different in every sample, and we never know it, because we never know the estimand.
  • SD (standard deviation) measures the spread of any list of numbers: wages in one sample, or slopes from 1,000 samples.
  • SE (standard error) is one particular SD: the SD of the estimate across many samples. It's the typical size of the sampling error. We only ever have one sample, so we estimate the SE (R does it for us).

So SD ≠ SE: the SD of wages in Card's data says how spread out wages are, not how much the slope bounces. And sampling error ≠ SE: the sampling error is how far off this sample is, and the SE is how far off a sample typically is.

Bias vs. sampling error: bias pushes the estimate the same way in every sample, and more data won't fix it. Sampling error is random around the truth and shrinks as samples grow.

Check yourself. With samples of 1,600 men (16 times as many as 100), about what would the SE be?

Show answer

16 times the data divides the SE by √16 = 4: 0.016 / 4 = 0.004.

A7: why does +2 count as the "bigger" error?

You asked

"Regression Day 3 (revisited), A7: Why is an error (e) that's equally far away from the true point (for example +2 and −2) treated as if +2 has the larger error? Is it not determined by absolute value?"

Good catch on the wording. "Bigger e" means more positive, not farther from zero. Think of temperatures: +2° and −2° are the same distance from 0, but +2° is warmer.

The sign is the whole point. e = +2 means the student reported more hours than they really had; e = −2 means fewer. Thumb 2 asks whether e moves up with reported hours, and taking the absolute value would erase exactly that direction.

In A7, P and Q both report 5 hours. P (e = +2) truly had 3 hours and scored 84. Q (e = −2) truly had 7 and scored 76. At the same reported hours, a more positive e goes with a higher score, so thumb 1 is +.

Part B: no bias, but less precise

You asked

"Day 3 revisited, Part B: What does it mean by no bias, but less precise? An example that includes other steps."

Picture darts. Bias means aiming at the wrong spot: every throw lands off the same way. Imprecision means aiming at the right spot, but the throws scatter more.

Random noise in log wages is the second kind. Averaged over many samples, the slope still lands on 0.052 (no bias). But each sample's slope bounces more, so the standard error grows: from 0.0029 to 0.0040 in Card's data.

New example, every step

A grader adds a random amount, up to 5 points either way, to each exam score. Does that bias the slope of score on study hours?

  1. Write it down. reported score = true score + v, where v is the random grading noise.
  2. Plug it into the true model. reported score = β0 + β1 hours + (u + v). The noise lands in the leftover.
  3. Two thumbs for v. v → reported score: +. v with hours: 0, because the noise is random, unrelated to hours and to u. Bias = (+) × 0 = 0.
  4. What changes. The leftover u + v is bigger, so the points scatter more around the line, so the slope wobbles more from sample to sample: a bigger SE.

Compare noise in x (Part A): that noise moves with reported x, so thumb 2 isn't zero, and on average it pulls the slope toward zero.

Robust standard errors: what, why and how

You asked

"…and ALSO exactly why we use robust SEs."

"How do you find the robust SE? With another example."

"Define robust S.E."

"Can you explain heteroskedasticity… and how it relates to regressions / why is it important?"

Define. A standard error is how much an estimate would bounce from sample to sample. R's default formula for it makes an assumption: that the points are spread around the line equally at every x. That's called homoskedasticity. When the spread changes with x (the cloud fans out), it's called heteroskedasticity. Homoskedasticity is an assumption about the process that produced the data. We only ever see one sample, so we can't know for sure whether it holds.

A robust standard error is computed without that assumption. If the spread really is equal, robust and default come out about the same (Card: 0.00287 vs. 0.00291). If it isn't, only the robust one is right. Since we can't know which is true, robust is the safe choice either way, and that's why we always report it.

Heteroskedasticity doesn't bias the slope. It makes the default SE wrong, and every t, p and interval built from that SE inherits the mistake.

How do you find it? You don't compute it by hand. R does it: modelsummary(m, vcov = "robust"), or lm_robust() in place of lm(). In a table, look for the note "Robust standard errors in parentheses," or, if you made the table, make sure you add that note!

Real or big: how we decide

You asked

"Day 4, Question 2b: Still confused with the question: is it rather real or not, and is it big or not?"

"Day 4, Real & Big. Another example. Specific how we decide 'Real' & 'Big.'"

They're two separate questions, and you answer both from the 95% confidence interval.

  1. Build the interval: estimate ± 1.96 × SE.
  2. Real? (statistically significant) Does the interval exclude 0? Excludes 0: real. Includes 0: not real. But this is assuming the null hypothesis is 0.
  3. Big? (economically significant) First say the smallest effect that would matter, and why. No formula picks it; it's a judgment call that depends on the setting. Then: if the whole interval is past that line, yes. If the whole interval is short of it, no. If the interval crosses it, can't tell.

"Not statistically significant" ("not real") can mean two different things. A tight interval around zero says the effect is near zero. A wide interval that includes zero says the data can't tell.

Dummies and interactions

You asked

"What does the coefficient on a dummy / interaction mean?"

A dummy's coefficient is the difference in average y between the group (dummy = 1) and the base group (dummy = 0), holding the other x's constant if we have other x's included. With no other x's, the intercept is the base group's average.

An interaction's coefficient is how much the slope differs between the groups.

New example: a coffee shop's daily sales
sales = 500 + 200 weekend

Weekdays average $500 (the intercept). Weekends average 500 + 200 = $700. The 200 is the gap between the two averages.

sales = 300 + 10 temp + 100 weekend + 5 (temp × weekend)

On weekdays each degree adds about $10. On weekends it adds about 10 + 5 = $15. The 5 is the difference in slopes between weekends and weekdays, in terms of how temperature is associated with sales.
